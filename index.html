Operating model · Head of Development · tech-education nonprofit

The operating model

Building the operating model behind a growing portfolio

How a development function grew with its portfolio: ownership, a shared lifecycle and infrastructure that many products can lean on.

Status
Operating practices in use · Capability model evolving
My role
Development leadership, portfolio priorities and coordination across specialist teams, leadership and field operations.

The challenge

Expanding a technology education portfolio adds more than content. Each new offering brings an audience, instructors, support needs, systems and measurement requirements. When several products depend on the same people and expertise, growth also requires a change in how work is managed.

My contribution

I led the development function as its programs and products expanded. My work included team structure, priorities, work planning and delivery reviews. I connected leadership, domain leads, technology and content teams, and field operations.

The choices that shaped the work

We developed a product vocabulary for learning programs: audience, need, value, delivery model and improvement cycle. The development lifecycle connected definition and production with delivery, debriefing and adaptation.

We also established specialist and product responsibilities alongside shared infrastructure. As the portfolio evolved, we began shaping cross-program expertise so that knowledge developed in one track could support others. This capability model continued to develop through practice.

What changed

The portfolio expanded, the team structure evolved and dedicated leadership roles were added. Alongside those changes, we defined a shared development lifecycle and gave the relationship between content, systems and measurement an explicit place in the function’s work.

The result was a clearer portfolio structure: domain ownership, shared infrastructure and a path for delivery insights to return to planning. The cross-program expertise model continued to develop in response to emerging needs.

What I learned

Growth increases dependencies between teams. Local ownership and shared capabilities have to develop together. The leadership decision is where to give a team room to adapt, and where a common standard helps the whole organization work together.

Pilot · Head of Development · tech-education nonprofit

AI content, with a quality bar

Designing quality into AI-assisted content production

A production pilot for AI-assisted learning materials, built around one question: when is something that looks finished actually ready to use?

Status
Pilot and process design · Broader rollout not established
My role
Pilot initiation, output standards and review across content, pedagogy and design.

The challenge

A lesson needs to exist as a presentation, learner material, an instructor guide and practice. Producing each independently adds editing work and creates consistency risks. AI can accelerate generation, but a polished artifact does not establish technical accuracy or teaching usability.

My contribution

I initiated a pilot to rework existing learning materials with AI and defined the need for a repeatable production process. I called for comparative review: what was preserved, changed or added, and which issues travelled between outputs. The review used AI tools alongside documented technical checks.

The choices that shaped the work

We designed a shared content foundation from which different outputs could be produced while retaining their distinct purposes: live instruction, independent reading, practice and instructor decision support.

Review extended to the source material itself. Faithfully reproducing an error can spread it across several formats. Requirements covered concepts and examples, alignment between goals and tasks, rendering, and separation of questions from answers. These were process requirements, not evidence of an already complete automated system.

What the pilot produced

The work produced revised artifacts, production instructions and a detailed comparative review. The review identified improvements in organization and consistency alongside conceptual errors, assessment gaps and display problems. Findings became correction requirements and acceptance criteria for subsequent iterations.

The outcome at this stage is a more explicit quality process. Consistent time savings, improved learning outcomes and completion of all corrections have not yet been established.

What I learned

The easier it becomes to generate something that looks finished, the more clearly we need to define readiness for use. Working with AI sharpened the distinction between generation, review and release, with evidence required at each stage.

Team design · Head of Development · tech-education nonprofit

From the field to the build team

Turning field experience into product capability

Bringing experienced instructors into development, and building the support channels that carry field questions back to the people who own the content.

Status
Hiring and support practices in use · Model evolving
My role
Development management, team design and prioritization of pathways from instruction into development.

The challenge

Instructors see where content meets a person: what makes sense, what causes confusion and what needs adapting. A development team needs that knowledge. At the same time, technical expertise and teaching experience do not automatically create the ability to build materials that other people can deliver consistently.

My contribution

In shaping the team, I prioritized internal recruitment of experienced, high-performing instructors into content and systems development. As leadership roles expanded, we also developed connections between development and delivery: office hours, individual support, curriculum adjustments and professional discussion spaces.

My contribution was the organizational connection between these roles and a shared professional standard. Domain leads and their teams built and delivered the work.

The choices that shaped the work

Knowledge of learners became part of how we built the team. This brought delivery experience into development alongside specialist expertise.

Support combined group responses to recurring questions, individual help for specific needs and proactive follow-up where a gap was visible. That connection helped teams respond to differences between groups and brought concrete questions back into development. Mentoring and internal learning routines were also identified as an area for further development.

What changed

Recruitment from the instructor community, changes in team structure and an expanded support offering created an explicit connection between field experience and development responsibilities. Experienced instructors gained a place in the teams building learning materials, while delivery teams gained channels for professional support.

The capability is organizational: delivery experience has a route into development, and field questions have a route to domain owners. Further development of internal mentoring was identified as a next step.

What I learned

People development can strengthen how an organization learns from delivery. A meaningful transition connects the experience a person brings with the performance standard and support their new role requires. Internal recruitment is the beginning of that process.

Learning product · Rafael · R&D and Safety

Safety Escape Room

Safety training you can't pass by guessing

A digital escape room for safety engineers. Hazards are randomized so the room tests mastery rather than memory, and passing triggers the completion certificate automatically.

The problem

Mandatory safety training tends to measure attendance, not understanding. A fixed quiz can be passed by remembering the answers.

What I built

A digital escape room for Rafael's safety engineers. Each run randomizes the hazards, so the only way out is to recognise them. A pass threshold gates the exit, and completion issues the certificate automatically, with no manual step.

Why it matters

The same principle as PLAYGROUND: put the learner inside the situation, and let the structure of the game do the assessing.

Program · STUFF.CO · for Elbit Systems

My Turn

Career design as a retention strategy

A career design program for post-COVID attrition — toolkits that help managers keep people, and an employee portal for growth and learning.

The problem

After COVID, people were leaving. The usual answer — more benefits — doesn't touch the reason most people go: they can't see their next step where they are.

What we designed

Two sides of the same program. For managers, toolkits for retention conversations and job crafting. For employees, a portal for growth and learning, so the next step inside the company is visible.

The idea underneath

Retention is a product problem: make the internal path easier to see and take than the external one.
