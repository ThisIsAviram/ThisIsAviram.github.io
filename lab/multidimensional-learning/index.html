Learning in more than one dimension

Interdisciplinary learning in middle school: why this is the right age to teach across disciplines, and what the evidence does — and doesn’t — support.

Written in Hebrew as a position paper for Israel's Ministry of Education. This is an English adaptation. The references were re-checked in October 2026, and a few claims were narrowed to match what the sources actually show.

Why middle school

Between roughly 12 and 15, students are building the capacity for abstract, systemic and spatial thinking. That makes it a good age to stop treating subjects as sealed rooms. Interdisciplinary learning brings the sciences, the humanities and the arts together around one question, and there is a long practice and research tradition behind treating it as an effective way to build the capabilities students will need (Drake & Reid, 2018, 2020).

This paper looks at the approaches that make integrated learning work, and at where technology and AI fit in.

Thinking in systems

Systems thinking is the ability to see how parts of a system affect one another over time: feedback, delays, side-effects (Sterman, 2000). It doesn't develop by itself inside a single subject. It needs problems that cross subjects. Schools have been bringing systems-thinking activities into K-12 classrooms across disciplines for two decades (Fisher, 2023). Spatial and visual media, including immersive ones, are a promising way to make those systems visible. That is still a design hypothesis worth testing, not a settled result.

Approaches that already work

Phenomenon-based learning. Finland's 2014 national core curriculum requires every school to run at least one multidisciplinary learning module a year for every student. Each module is built around a real phenomenon, and students take part in planning it (Finnish National Agency for Education, 2016). A topic such as sustainability or migration is studied from several angles at once, and understanding comes from the connections between them.

STEAM. Adding the arts to STEM matters for memory as well as motivation. In a controlled classroom study, arts-integrated science units produced the same initial learning as conventional ones and better retention on a delayed test. The largest gains came for students who were weaker readers (Hardiman, Rinne & Yarmolinskaya, 2014).

Project-based learning and design thinking. PBL asks students to solve a real problem as a team, over time. In a large randomized study in elementary science, a PBL curriculum produced significant gains in achievement and in reflection, ownership and collaboration (Krajcik et al., 2023). Design thinking offers teachers a method for building interdisciplinary units in the first place (Wang, 2024).

Computational thinking and AI literacy

Computational thinking means breaking a problem down, abstracting and recognizing patterns. It is a way of thinking, not a subject. A systematic review of how K-12 schools integrate it found that most integration happens in science and mathematics, often at a shallow "substitution" level (Yeni et al., 2024). The opportunity is to take it further, into the humanities and the arts.

AI literacy is quickly becoming a basic literacy. Students arrive with their own conceptions of what AI is, and teaching has to start from those conceptions (Mertala, Fagerlund & Calderon, 2022). Project-based AI literacy courses for secondary students have shown that conceptual understanding, ethical judgment and hands-on building can be taught together (Kong, Cheung & Tsang, 2024). AI literacy belongs across the curriculum, because its questions are technical, social and ethical at the same time.

What leading systems do

Finland, Canada, Australia and the United States all make room for cross-curricular learning in their curricula, each in its own way. Finland builds it into national requirements. Parts of Canada weave Indigenous knowledge into multidisciplinary study. In the US, programs such as the Big History Project teach history, science and technology as one story (OECD, 2019).

The argument

The evidence points one way. Learning that combines disciplines, connects ideas spatially and visually, and uses technology and AI as subjects of inquiry builds critical thinking, creativity and metacognition at the age when those capacities are taking shape.

The evidence is not uniform. Reviews of interdisciplinary education still describe parts of the research base as inconclusive (Warkentien et al., 2022). That is a reason to adopt these models as a foundation for curriculum design deliberately, with measurement built in from the first pilot.
