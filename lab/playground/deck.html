PLAYGROUND

Terms

Game-based learning ≠ gamification

GAME-BASED LEARNINGThe game is the vehicle

A complete game designed to deliver and embed content, balancing subject matter, play, and the learner’s ability to apply it in the real world.

GAMIFICATIONGame elements in a non-game context

Game-design techniques, game thinking and mechanics applied inside an existing process to drive adoption and change behaviour (Kapp, 2012; Deterding et al., 2011).

PLAYGROUND

Framework

Meaningful gamification, not reward-based. Nicholson’s six principles (2014).

01 · PLAY

Freedom to explore, try and fail within agreed boundaries.

02 · EXPOSITION

A narrative that connects moments and puts the learner at the centre.

03 · CHOICE

Control over path, order and mode of work — with scaffolding.

04 · INFORMATION

The why and the how — not just “how many points”.

05 · ENGAGEMENT

Social connection and flow: challenge that grows with skill.

06 · REFLECTION

Looking back and linking the experience to real life.

PLAYGROUND

Design idea

The course is built from the principles it teaches. Each principle is a level that earns a “gamification stone”.

The course ran inside Classcraft — a digital role-playing game for classroom management: avatars, powers, teams and a framing story. Teachers entered as players, collected six stones, and only after each level received the theory behind it.

  • Play — solve a puzzle01
  • Exposition — search a map02
  • Choice — which door?03
  • Information — podcast, article, image04
  • Engagement — a group video05
  • Reflection — writing06
PLAYGROUND

Activity sequence

Five meetings: from playing, through principles, to the classroom.

  1. Meeting gamesAn educational vs. a commercial game; reflection as player and observer.
  2. Principles, part 1Entering Classcraft. Play · Exposition · Choice.
  3. Principles, part 2Information · Engagement · Reflection. Wrap-up via Monopoly.
  4. Building a unitEach teacher builds a lesson around one principle, then adds a second.
  5. Open dayPresenting the lessons taught in class; peer feedback.

PLAYGROUND

Findings · 1–2

Prior experience set the pace — but everyone finished feeling successful.

PRIOR EXPERIENCE

Teachers with digital-game experience stood out, showed self-efficacy and helped others. Those without it struggled more — mostly with platform onboarding — yet left with a basic understanding and a sense of success.

VALUE OF THE COURSE

Four of six reported success and enthusiasm; two — the oldest in the group — felt overloaded. Across the board: “this will stay with me,” and above all, the principles work without technology.

PLAYGROUND

Findings · 3–4

Views shifted for some. Students, teachers reported, enjoyed it more.

CHANGE IN VIEWS

Two already used gamification; two changed their views; two veterans with 25+ years said they would not use the principles regularly.

STUDENT MOTIVATION

Lessons with at least one principle were seen as more enjoyable and energetic. By the end, teachers used more gamification principles than digital tools — regardless of prior experience.

From the field

I’ve never finished a computer game in my life — what fun!

A participant, after the third meeting. In the closing interview, another: “You didn’t force the computer on us.”

PLAYGROUND

Limits & implications

A small sample, a directional finding: train teachers through experience, not lectures.

Only six participants

Not generalisable. On the other hand, facilitators were fully available.

No credit or reward

On their own time; content was sometimes shortened. Commitment held.

Proximity to a researcher

One facilitator coordinated the participants’ grade level.
